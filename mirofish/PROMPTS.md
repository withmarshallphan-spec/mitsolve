# MiroFish prompts: MIT Solve 2027, starting from scratch

**How to use**
1. Upload `reality_seed.pdf` (or `reality_seed.md`) as the seed material.
2. Run **Prompt 0** first for the full end-to-end analysis.
3. Run Prompts 1–14 one at a time if you want each issue examined in depth. Each prompt stands on its own.

**Neutrality.** The prompts deliberately contain no earlier ideas, candidate solutions or conclusions. They ask the questions only. The seed contains facts and the applicant's stated priorities, labelled [APPLICANT].

**Suggested simulated population.** Paste this into any prompt if MiroFish asks who should take part:
> MIT Solve judges with varied backgrounds (health, education, labour and economic inclusion, climate, technology, investment); Solve staff screeners; Batam residents across occupations, ages and income levels; Indonesian practitioners in education, health, labour, climate and digital services; local and provincial government officials; NGO staff; members of past Solver teams; and the applicant. Agents must ground factual claims in the seed and label anything else as their own speculation.

**Rules to add at the end of any prompt (recommended)**
> Ground every factual claim in the seed and say which seed section it comes from. Label inferences and simulated opinions as such. Where the seed is silent or contradictory, say so instead of filling the gap. Consider at least three alternatives before narrowing. Report disagreement between agents rather than averaging it away.

## Prompt 0: Master question (run first)

> Using only the seed material, work out the strongest possible MIT Solve 2027 Global Challenge application this specific applicant could submit before the deadline. Work through these steps in order:
> 1. Which challenge and dimension.
> 2. Which specific, real problem.
> 3. Which solution actually solves it.
> 4. How the applicant can validate it with real people in the time available.
> 5. What working prototype can be built in time.
> 6. How the application should be framed.
>
> Simulate how Solve's judges would score the result against Solve's stated criteria, and estimate its chances honestly. If no option is strong enough to be worth submitting this cycle, say so and explain what the applicant should do instead.

## Prompt 1: Deadline and eligibility

> The seed contains conflicting deadline information and conflicting statements about which development stages are eligible.
> 1. What is certain, what is uncertain, and what must the applicant verify directly, in what order, before doing anything else?
> 2. Given the applicant's situation, which stage label is accurate and allowed?
> 3. What eligibility risks could disqualify the application?

## Prompt 2: Choosing the challenge and dimension

> Using the four 2027 Global Challenges and their dimensions, decide which challenge and dimension give this applicant the best realistic chance. Weigh fit with the applicant's location, resources, reach and timeline; the strength of evidence that a problem exists in the applicant's context; the competition likely in each challenge; and the partner prizes the applicant could realistically be eligible for. Rank all four challenges and explain the ranking.

## Prompt 3: What selected teams have in common

> Using the lists of past selected teams and prize winners, plus Solve's stated judging criteria and selection rates, identify what distinguishes selected solutions from the rest of the applicant pool. Then simulate the judging panel: how would judges weigh team experience, closeness to the affected community, stage of development, evidence of impact, and novelty for a solo, prototype-stage applicant? Which disadvantages can such an applicant realistically overcome, and which can't they?

## Prompt 4: Finding the problem

> The applicant has not identified a problem from personal experience. Using the seed's facts on Batam, the Riau Islands and Indonesia, generate a broad list of candidate problems across all four challenges. Do not narrow early. For each candidate, state:
> - the evidence of the problem;
> - who is affected and how many;
> - whether the applicant can reach the affected people.
>
> Include problems the applicant might not think of. Then shortlist 3–5, giving clear reasons for each inclusion and each exclusion.

## Prompt 5: Is the problem real and serious?

> For each shortlisted problem:
> 1. Is it a real, existing and widespread problem in society, backed by the seed's data? Or is it an inconvenience, or a problem only in theory?
> 2. How serious is the harm, and to whom?
> 3. What would happen if nothing were done?
>
> Flag any problem whose evidence in the seed is thin, unverified or contradictory.

## Prompt 6: Does the solution remove the cause, or just help around it?

> The applicant wants a solution that actually solves a problem, not one that merely "helps" people who still have to overcome the real obstacle themselves. The applicant uses OkHi (a 2025 selected team) as a reference: a definite problem (addresses not represented digitally) and a definite solution (those addresses become represented and valid).
>
> For each shortlisted problem:
> 1. Identify the root cause or binding constraint behind the harm, using the seed's evidence.
> 2. Propose solutions and judge whether each removes that constraint or works around it.
> 3. Explain what outcome would change, for whom, and how you would see it change.
>
> Point out any proposal that addresses a side issue rather than the main cause.

## Prompt 7: Existing solutions, incumbents and novelty

> For each proposed solution, use the seed's landscape section to identify existing tools, government programmes and past Solve teams that already do the same thing or something close. Judge whether the proposal creates a genuinely new capability or is a feature of something that already exists. State what would make it defensible, or say that it is not defensible.

## Prompt 8: Validation and reach

> The applicant is solo, reaches people mainly through WhatsApp and personal networks, and finds that local institutions rarely answer email. The applicant notes that strong past applicants had already reached the people or organisations who would use their solution.
> 1. For each proposal, what validation evidence can this applicant realistically gather from real people before the deadline?
> 2. What would Solve's judges count as credible validation at prototype stage?
> 3. Is the local communication culture a disadvantage, or can it be used as an asset?
> 4. Which proposals require approval or data from institutions that the applicant cannot obtain in time?

## Prompt 9: Can beneficiaries actually access and use it?

> For each proposal:
> - Can the people who most need it actually access it? Use the seed's data on phone ownership, internet access, urban–rural gaps and income.
> - Do they have the time, ability and motivation to use it?
> - Who would it reach first, and who would it miss?
>
> Consider whether the poorest or most remote people would be excluded, and whether that undermines the claimed impact.

## Prompt 10: Data needs and reality check

> For each proposal:
> 1. What data does it need to work?
> 2. Where would that data come from?
> 3. Can the applicant obtain it legally and ethically?
> 4. How accurate is it likely to be?
> 5. Does the solution depend on information that doesn't exist or can't be measured?
> 6. Which parts would be real, and which would have to be simulated or invented in a prototype?

## Prompt 11: What working prototype is possible in time?

> For the strongest proposals, define the smallest prototype that is actually working, practical and real, and that the applicant can build and put in front of real users before the deadline. Use the seed's technical facts, including the WhatsApp Cloud API test-number limit. State:
> - what it does end to end;
> - what is genuinely functional and what is a stand-in;
> - what the applicant needs to set it up;
> - how its working would be shown to judges.

## Prompt 12: Framing and name

> For the strongest proposal, write the problem statement so that it leads with the problem rather than the tool. Then propose several names that tell a reader immediately what problem is being solved. Note any name that is unclear, misleading, or too close to an existing product.

## Prompt 13: Honest odds, and apply now or wait?

> Simulate the full Solve review (screening, judge scoring, finalist stage) for the strongest proposal as this applicant could realistically present it by the deadline. Estimate the chances of passing each stage, using the seed's selection rates. Name the single biggest weakness judges would identify.
>
> Then compare two options:
> - (a) submitting this cycle;
> - (b) using the time to build real validation and a team and applying in the next cycle.
>
> Weigh the trade-offs and give a clear answer.

## Prompt 14: Final synthesis

> Bring together the results of the previous prompts into one recommendation:
> - the challenge and dimension;
> - the problem statement, with the evidence and its sources in the seed;
> - the solution, and why it addresses the root cause;
> - existing alternatives, and why this one is different;
> - the validation plan for the time left;
> - the prototype specification;
> - impact metrics;
> - the main risks and how to mitigate them;
> - honest odds.
>
> List every assumption that the applicant must verify in the real world before submitting.
